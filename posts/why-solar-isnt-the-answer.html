Why Solar Isn't the Long-Term Answer

Originally published on Substack.

With energy usage soaring due to the increasing adoption of electric vehicles, artificial intelligence, and a world becoming increasingly dependent on electricity, we are entering a period where the correct type of energy development is becoming crucial. Look around you; has your energy use increased in the past few years? How about those around you? This demand for energy needs to be addressed in the right way. The current state of renewable energy has been led by solar, which has been an essential stepping stone to a more permanent solution. It has been and continues to be a pivotal piece of the puzzle as new technologies are developed to solve the energy problem, however, solar struggles with volatility, compatibility, and scalability. When discussing solar volatility, the National Renewable Energy Laboratory (NREL) stated, "both variability and uncertainty can create challenges for grid operators when balancing electricity generation with demand while obeying power system constraints and maintaining the lowest possible costs" (National Renewable Energy Laboratory, 2013).

Although solar has played a crucial role in solidifying the validity of renewable energy, it has too many issues with compatibility, scalability, and volatility to be a long-term solution. On a small scale, an area where I have personally seen solar fall short is in residential solar. I installed solar panels in 2022 and have been using them for a few years. During that time, there were constraints on the amount of energy a single home could produce because it was causing too much inconsistency within the grid. Yes, this issue goes beyond simply making more energy. There are fundamental issues with the grid, as well as with energy storage, however, due to these problems and the fact that they do not appear to be getting resolved anytime soon, solar energy is not a long-term solution.

The inherent volatility of solar makes it extremely difficult for the grid to handle its inputs. Battery storage would be excellent, but the technology is lacking. The environmentally friendly and inexpensive systems are highly inefficient, and the efficient systems are expensive and detrimental to the environment, both in terms of material acquisition and disposal. I believe this technology will catch up, but that's a discussion for another time. The problem solar has is its inconsistent production. This leads to a high dependency on energy storage, placing significant stress on the grid.

Solar energy is inconsistent for several reasons, but first and foremost, it relies on the sun being out to produce energy. For half of the day, energy is not being generated. In many areas of the country, the sun is not out enough to produce even that much energy at any given time, which further leads to challenges with reliability nationwide. A prime example of how this can cause large-scale problems is California's duck curve. As the U.S. Energy Information Administration discussed in the article As Solar Capacity Grows, Duck Curves Are Getting Deeper In California, "The extreme swing in demand for electricity from conventional power plants from midday to late evenings, when energy demand is still high but solar generation has dropped off, means that conventional power plants (such as natural gas-fired plants) must quickly ramp up electricity production to meet consumer demand" (U.S. Energy Information Administration, 2022). We will need an energy source that can consistently meet this demand. Even if we had the grid infrastructure and storage technology, solar power would still be unable to guarantee 24/7 power.

Additionally, solar is not scalable without significant trade-offs. On a high level, there is too much energy loss from energy transport, particularly when large solar farms transport energy a long distance to their final destination. The land usage of these large solar farms is inefficient and can harm local ecosystems and occupy a considerable amount of space, resulting in inefficient resource utilization. Rooftop solar is a fantastic way to address this problem on a small scale and should continue to be utilized as a lever in the transition to alternative energy sources. It can be quickly deployed, its materials are widely available, and costs have declined dramatically over the past few decades. For these reasons, solar energy will never entirely disappear. It has some incredible applications and should continue to be leveraged. However, solar will not be the solution to the energy problem we are experiencing today. With the electrification of more products, such as vehicles, and the increasing use of artificial intelligence, solar energy alone will not be sufficient to meet this demand. There will be a need for a more reliable source of renewable energy that operates with greater consistency, compatibility, and scalability.
